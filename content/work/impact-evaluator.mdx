A DAO can agree to reward contributions and still have a difficult question left: who runs the calculation, and why should everyone trust the result?

Impact Evaluator explored a way to make that calculation reproducible. Contributors recorded data on Ceramic. A DAO supplied its own scoring function. Bacalhau ran that function in a container, and its output became a reward allocation that could be settled on-chain.

I built the off-chain compute layer while working at Curve Labs, on a project funded by a Protocol Labs grant to the studio. My responsibility covered the job runner, Docker packaging, reward-tree generation, execution scripts, and the template a DAO could fork to write its evaluator. Other engineers built the Ceramic data model, settlement contracts, and application interface.

## Let each DAO define its own calculation

I separated the scoring function from the machinery that ran it. A DAO could edit one function returning rewards and a new trusted seed, then build its image on top of the shared runner.

That boundary mattered. Changing a reward policy should not require rebuilding the job orchestration or learning the whole infrastructure stack. I moved the runner toward a single-execution model with one input file, and made previous rewards optional so the template was useful beyond the example calculation.

## Make the output safe to hand over

The runner checked the function's return shape and stopped with an explicit error when execution failed or returned invalid output. That catches broken integrations before they become an allocation file; it does not establish whether a DAO's scoring policy is fair.

I then converted the allocation into an OpenZeppelin Merkle tree with address-and-amount leaves matching the settlement contract. The tree and reward files were concrete outputs of the job. The on-chain system could commit to one root while contributors claimed with their individual proofs.

## Package the whole path

I wrote the scripts to launch Bacalhau jobs, retrieve their results, and run against IPFS or HTTPS inputs. I also created the forkable template, its authoring documentation, and a recorded walkthrough, then used my own fork to demonstrate the customization flow.

The complete system was demonstrated on Goerli, including settlement through a Safe and Zodiac Reality module. It remained a prototype. The result of my work was a reusable compute pipeline and a documented way for another developer to supply an evaluator and carry its output through to settlement.
