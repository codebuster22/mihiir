Oracl explored a specific prediction-market idea: the people who create a market and bring traders to it should participate in the fees it generates.

I designed and built the Solidity protocol prototype, its specification and tests, and the investor-demo application. The work covered both the market's economic rules and the screens someone would use to create, trade and resolve it.

## Turning the idea into a protocol

I separated the contract system into a factory, an individual market contract and an ERC-1155 conditional-token layer. The factory creates markets and controls resolution. Each market handles pricing and trading. The token layer represents its YES and NO positions, with USDC as the intended collateral.

For pricing, I implemented the Logarithmic Market Scoring Rule, or LMSR. This gives a binary market a pricing function based on the outstanding shares and its liquidity parameter. Translating that function into Solidity meant working with fixed-point approximations and a binary search to calculate how many shares a trade could buy.

I also wrote a functional specification that made the intended accounting rules explicit: prices should sum correctly, fee distributions should conserve value, and resolution should follow the permitted authority and lifecycle.

## Making attribution part of the trade

The promoter address enters through the trade call. The contract records the corresponding fee allocation alongside the trade, rather than relying on a separate referral database to reconcile it afterward.

I used accrued balances that recipients withdraw themselves. That keeps recipient transfers outside the trading transaction and reduces the external interactions involved in executing a trade. The architecture makes creator and promoter participation part of the protocol's accounting model.

## Making the product explorable early

The investor demo needed to show a complete journey before the contracts were deployed. I built market discovery, trading, creation, positions and creator, promoter and admin views against a simulated backend.

The pricing preview and simulated purchases share the same TypeScript LMSR implementation. Server-sent events update market activity, while batched cache writes keep those updates from triggering unnecessary renders. API and data-access layers isolate the simulation so a future backend can replace it without rebuilding the screens.

## What was delivered

I delivered the demo application and a reference contract system. The demo simulates market activity and transactions; it is not connected to deployed trading contracts.

The contracts remain an unaudited prototype with unresolved accounting and lifecycle issues, including fee allocation and voided-market redemption. They were never deployed on-chain. This case demonstrates the product and protocol design work, with production hardening still required before the system could handle funds.
