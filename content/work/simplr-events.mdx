Turning a ticket into a token does not settle the difficult parts of ticketing. A buyer still needs to know the ticket is valid. Its QR code needs to stay private. The seller needs a way to receive payment, and the person at the gate needs to reject a ticket that has already been used.

Simplr Events was our own product at Chain Labs, supported by an Arbitrum grant. It evolved from email-based ticket claims into a resale protocol, then into a system covering primary sales and venue redemption.

I owned the smart-contract architecture and protocol implementation, built the indexers, and handled the later applications' Web3 integration. I also wrote the original email-claim backend. Teammates built the main interfaces and subsequent API servers.

## Keep private ticket data off the public ledger

For resale, I designed a digital representation of an existing ticket. Event-specific verification checked the seller's claim to the original ticket. Sensitive contents were encrypted with Lit Protocol and stored on IPFS; the contract held a reference and a hash of the serial number.

The verification interface let each event supply its own ownership check. The Moongate implementation combined a signature with a check against the original ticket contract, keeping that event-specific logic outside the marketplace.

## Model the awkward part of a sale

Sellers signed listings off-chain. A purchase moved the ticket and seller proceeds into escrow, with release following buyer confirmation. Either party could raise a dispute, and an administrator could resolve it.

I made access to the encrypted ticket depend on the escrow state. The same lifecycle governing payment therefore informed who could read the ticket. I also built a Ponder indexer that discovered newly created event contracts and turned their transactions into queryable ticket, listing, and escrow records for the applications.

## Treat admission as its own protocol

In the later QIE version, I introduced ticket tiers and signed QR redemption with a deadline and a per-user nonce. Redemption consumed the ticket and issued an access pass with a temporary transfer restriction. The scanner's preflight checks were batched into one RPC request.

The work produced several generations of contracts, indexing, and integration documentation. We executed an integration for Devcon. The later venue-redemption work extended the engineering from buying and reselling a ticket to checking it at the gate, with privacy, payment and admission each represented in the system.
